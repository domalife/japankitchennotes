Japanese kitchen knives, explained

A Japanese knife is described by its shape, its steel, how it was forged and where. Each of those changes how it cuts and how much care it needs. Here is what the words on the box mean.

Which knife shape?

Four shapes cover almost every home kitchen.

Santoku and gyuto overlap. Pick by how you cut, not by what is on sale.

Where knives are made

The town on the box tells you how it was built.

Sakai 堺 (Osaka)
Forging, sharpening and handles done by separate specialists
Seki 関 (Gifu)
Large-scale production, stainless, consistent
Sanjo / Tsubame 三条・燕
Niigata metalworking district, many small smiths
Echizen 越前 (Fukui)
Hand-forged tradition, over 700 years
Tosa 土佐 (Kochi)
Free-forged, rustic finish, farm-tool roots
Miki 三木 (Hyogo)
Long-established tool and blade town

Sakai splits the work between craftsmen. That is why one knife carries several names.

Single bevel or double bevel?

This decides whether you can actually sharpen it.

Double bevel両刃Single bevel片刃

Single bevel knives are specialist tools. Buy one because you want that job done, not as a first knife.

Which whetstone grit?

Most people buy the wrong one first.

200-600 grit 荒砥
Repair chips, reset a bevel. Rarely needed
800-1200 grit 中砥
The one to buy first. Actual sharpening happens here
3000-6000 grit 仕上砥
Refines the edge. Optional for home use
8000 grit and up
Polishing. Diminishing returns for kitchen work

One 1,000-grit stone and a flattening plate will out-perform a four-stone set you never use.

Carbon steel without rust

Five habits. That is the whole maintenance routine.

Wipe while you work
Acidic food marks carbon steel in minutes
Hand wash only
A dishwasher will ruin the edge and the handle
Dry immediately
Do not leave it on the rack
Wood or plastic board
Glass and stone destroy the edge
Let the patina form
The grey film is protective, not damage

A blue-black patina is what a used carbon knife is supposed to look like.

Your first Japanese knife

Five decisions, in the order they actually matter.

Shape
Santoku or gyuto. Everything else is a second knife
Length
180mm santoku or 210mm gyuto suits most home kitchens
Steel
Stainless unless you want to wipe the blade as you cook
Bevel
Double bevel. Single bevel is a specialist tool
Handle
Japanese handles are lighter and easy to replace

Spending more on a shape you will not use is the most common first mistake.

A wa handle can be swapped when it wears out. That is why old knives keep working.

Kasumi, honyaki, san-mai

Damascus is a surface pattern on a clad blade. It describes the look, not the performance.

Sharpening on a stone

Five steps. The middle one is the one people skip.

Soak the stone
Ten minutes, until bubbles stop, unless it is a splash-and-go
Hold about 15 degrees
Two coins under the spine is close enough
Raise a burr
Work one side until you feel a wire edge along the whole length
Flip and repeat
Same number of strokes, other side
Strop it off
Light alternating passes to remove the burr

If you never felt a burr, the edge was not sharpened. That is the step that does the work.

What to cut on

The board decides how long the edge lasts.

Hinoki 檜
Soft, kind to edges, naturally water-resistant
Ginkgo 銀杏
Traditional professional choice, very forgiving

Good, cheap, dishwasher-safe

Glass, stone, steel

A thin Japanese edge is ground for cutting food, not for surviving the board.
